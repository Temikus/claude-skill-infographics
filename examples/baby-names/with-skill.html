US baby names are fragmenting

Run 2026-09-25 · Social Security Administration data 1880–2008 · share of births by sex

In 2008 fewer than one in ten US babies got a top-10 name, down from one in three boys and one in four girls in 1950. Names outside the top 1,000 now cover 33% of girls, so a name says less about when someone was born.

  1. The top-10 share fell every decade since 1950

    Boys went from 33% to 9%, girls from 23% to 8%. Boys' names were always more concentrated, and the gap has now almost closed.

  2. Most of the 2008 top ten was new within a decade

    5 of the top-10 boys' names and 7 of the girls' names were outside the top ten in 1998. Alexander, Anthony, Daniel, Ethan and William displaced Andrew, Austin, Brandon, Nicholas and Tyler.

  3. A #1 name today is a tenth as common as in 1880

    John was given to 8.2% of boys in 1880. Jacob, the 2008 leader, got 1.0%. Mary peaked at 7.2%; Emma peaked at 2.1%.

Names now peak lower and fade faster

Share of births by sex for six #1 names · same scale on every panel · 1880–2008

Mary held #1 for most years until 1961. Jennifer took about 15 years to rise and 15 to fall. Jacob led for a decade without ever passing 1.8%. Emma's 2008 peak is a revival: it first peaked in 1881.

How reliable is this

The data counts Social Security card applications, not births. Registration was voluntary before 1937 and many people born earlier never applied, so shares before about 1920 rest on smaller counts and the early-1900s dip should not be read as a naming trend. The source lists only the top 1,000 names per sex per year, so "outside the top 1,000" is the remainder, and includes names given to fewer than five children, which SSA withholds. The series stops at 2008.

Method

Each row of the source is a name, sex, year and share of that sex's births. Top-10 share is the sum of the ten largest shares in a year. Top-1,000 share is the sum of all listed shares. Sparklines show 1880–2008, last point marked. Charts are inline SVG generated by build.py from data.json.

Source: SSA national baby names via hadley/data-baby-names, top 1,000 names per sex per year, 1880–2008.
